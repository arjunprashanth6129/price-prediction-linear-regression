\begin{abstract}
In this sudy, I test whether four technical features built from past prices, a 5-day, a
20-day moving average, the one-day return and the ratio of the two averages,
predict the next-day return of \NStocks{} stocks listed on India's National Stock
Exchange. A least squares model is estimated for each stock based on 
data from June 2019 to January 2025 and evaluated on the following seventeen months . 
Out of sample, the mean $R^2$ (coefficient of determination) is \RsqMean{} and
mean directional accuracy is \AccMean. No stock's accuracy is significantly above
50\%, and Pesaran-Timmermann tests, which allow for the base rate of up days,
find no stock with significant directional ability. The model's apparent edge over an always-up benchmark 
is smaller than the edge an uninformative forecast would have earned in the same window. The
results are consistent with weak-form of the efficient market hypothesis at the daily horizon.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Technical analysis rests on the premise that the recent path of a price says
something about its next move, and moving-average rules are its most common form.
In the best known version, a short moving average that rises above a long one, a
\emph{golden cross}, is read as a signal to buy, and the reverse crossing, a
\emph{death cross}, as a signal to sell. Figure~\ref{fig:cross} shows both in
Britannia Industries' share price in 2019: the 5-day average rose above the
20-day average on \CrossGolden{} and fell back below it on \CrossDeath. Almost
every charting platform draws these averages, and many retail investors trade on
them.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{figures/cross.pdf}
\caption{A golden cross and a death cross in Britannia Industries, August to
November 2019. The golden cross is the day where the 5-day moving average of the close
rises above the 20-day average; the death cross is the day it falls back below.
The ratio of the two averages, one of the four features in this study, is above
one between the two crossings and below one outside them.}
\label{fig:cross}
\end{figure}


The academic evidence on them is mixed and has weakened over time.
\citet{lo2000} put the visual patterns of technical analysis on a statistical
footing and find that several carry incremental information about returns.
\citet{brock1992} found that moving-average and trading-range rules applied to the
Dow Jones Industrial Average from 1897 to 1986 produced returns that standard null
models of prices could not explain; \citet{sullivan1999} re-examined the same
universe of rules with a bootstrap that corrects for data snooping and found that
the best rule's performance did not carry over into the following decade. In their
survey of 95 modern studies, \citet{park2007} report that most find profits until
the early 1990s, but that many are affected by data snooping, ex post selection of
rules and difficulties in measuring risk and transaction costs. For South Asian
markets, \citet{gunasekarage2001} report evidence of predictive ability for
moving-average rules in index data for four exchanges, including the Bombay Stock
Exchange. More generally, \citet{welch2008} show that most variables proposed as
predictors of equity returns fail to beat the historical mean once they are
evaluated on data not used to select them, and \citet{campbell2008} formalise that
comparison with the out-of-sample $R^2$.

This paper asks a narrow version of the question for a recent sample of Indian
stocks: given only a stock's recent price history, summarised by four standard
technical features, does a linear model forecast the sign and size of the next
day's return more accurately than benchmarks that use no model? The null
hypothesis is that the features carry no information about that return. The design
choices that determine whether the answer can be trusted were fixed before any
model was estimated. The split between estimation and evaluation is chronological,
the features are standardised with statistics from the estimation sample only,
the target is a return rather than a price, and the benchmarks against which the
model is judged were chosen in advance.

The answer is negative. Across \NStocks{} stocks, the mean out-of-sample $R^2$ is
\RsqMean, and \NRsqNeg{} stocks have a negative value. Mean directional accuracy is
\AccMean. The model exceeds an always-up benchmark for \NEdgePos{} stocks, by
\EdgeMean{} percentage points on average, but this reflects the benchmark rather
than the model: up days were scarce in the evaluation window, and a forecast with
no information would have exceeded the same benchmark by \RandomEdge{} points. No
stock's accuracy is significantly above 50\%, and the coefficient on the
moving-average ratio, the variable that encodes the crossing rule, is never the
largest of the four.

The paper makes three contributions. It provides out-of-sample evidence on
moving-average features for a recent, broad sample of Indian stocks across three
capitalisation bands. It shows how a weak always-up benchmark in a flat market can
manufacture an apparent edge, and that the appropriate comparison is the expected
edge of an uninformative forecast, which can be computed stock by stock. And it
documents, with a worked example, why a high $R^2$ on price levels says nothing
about forecasting ability.

I came to this question through an equity research programme at BugleRock
Capital, where the contrast between fundamental analysis and technical trading came
up repeatedly. Testing the most popular technical signal on data it had not seen
seemed the most direct way to find out how much the evidence supports it.

Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data,
Section~\ref{sec:method} the method and Section~\ref{sec:results} the results.
Section~\ref{sec:levels} presents the worked example, Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses the findings and Section~\ref{sec:limits} their limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}\label{sec:data}

The universe consists of \NUniverse{} companies listed on the National Stock
Exchange of India, chosen to span large, mid and small capitalisation. Each stock
is assigned to a capitalisation band as of June 2021. Daily closing prices from
1~June 2019 to 30~June 2026 were downloaded from Yahoo Finance and adjusted for
splits and bonus issues, which would otherwise appear as large one-day losses. The
sample period is fixed rather than trailing, so the dataset can be reproduced
exactly; the price file is committed with the code.

Figure~\ref{fig:universe} shows the sample. Over the estimation period the median
stock with a full history rose by a factor of \MedianFullGrowth, but over the
evaluation period the median stock ended almost where it began, with a change of
\MedianTestChange. That flat evaluation window matters later, because it leaves up
days in the minority for most stocks (Panel B) and so weakens the simplest
benchmark.

\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/universe.pdf}

Two stocks returned no usable history over the period and are excluded, leaving
\NStocks{} stocks. Of these, \NFull{} traded on every session in the period. The
other \NPartial{}, mostly companies listed after June 2019, use the history
available to them. After the features are constructed, the median stock has
\MedianObs{} daily observations, of which \MedianTrainObs{} are used for
estimation. The evaluation sample has a mean length of \MeanTestDays{} trading
days, from \MinTestDays{} for the most recent listing to \MaxTestDays.

Price adjustment does not correct for demergers. Two demergers fall inside the
evaluation window: Raymond on 14~May 2025 and Tata Motors on 14~October 2025. Each
produces a one-day price fall that is a corporate action rather than a market move.
Both stocks are retained and the issue is noted in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Features and target}

Let $P_t$ denote a stock's adjusted close on day $t$. The four features use closes
up to and including day $t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:features}
  \mathit{ma5}_t = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=0}^{4} P_{t-i}, \qquad
  \mathit{ma20}_t = \frac{1}{20}\sum_{i=0}^{19} P_{t-i}, \qquad
  r_t = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1, \qquad
  \mathit{ratio}_t = \frac{\mathit{ma5}_t}{\mathit{ma20}_t}.
\end{equation}
The ratio is above one when the short average is above the long one, the
golden-cross state, and below one in the death-cross state. Its distance from one
measures how far apart the averages are. Expressing the crossing condition as a
continuous variable allows the regression to assign it a coefficient, which a
binary crossing indicator that fires a few times a year would not support. The
target is the next day's return, $r_{t+1} = P_{t+1}/P_t - 1$. Section~\ref{sec:levels}
explains why the target is a return rather than a price.

\subsection{Estimation}

For each stock $i$, the model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model}
  r_{i,t+1} = \beta_{i0} + \sum_{k=1}^{4} \beta_{ik}\, \tilde x_{ik,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t+1}
\end{equation}
is estimated by ordinary least squares, where $\tilde x_{ik,t}$ is feature $k$
standardised to zero mean and unit variance. Standardisation makes the
coefficients comparable: each $\beta_{ik}$ is the change in the forecast return for
a one standard deviation change in the feature. The model has five parameters per
stock, which limits the scope for overfitting on roughly 1,400 estimation
observations, and its coefficients can be read directly. Estimation uses
scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}.

\subsection{Sample split and the flow of information}

The first 80\% of each stock's observations form the estimation sample and the
remaining 20\% the evaluation sample, in date order (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). Two
sources of look-ahead are excluded. A random split would place later observations
in the estimation sample and judge the model on earlier ones; it would also
evaluate the model on rows that are near duplicates of estimation rows, because
$\mathit{ma20}$ on consecutive days shares 19 of its 20 inputs. And the
standardisation parameters are computed from the estimation sample alone and then
applied to both samples, so the mean and variance of the evaluation period do not
enter the transform. Nothing is re-estimated during the evaluation sample.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Three statistics are computed on each stock's evaluation sample of $n_i$ days. The
\emph{out-of-sample} $R^2$ compares the model with a constant forecast,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:r2}
  R^2_{OS,i} = 1 - \frac{\sum_t \left(r_{i,t+1} - \hat r_{i,t+1}\right)^2}
                       {\sum_t \left(r_{i,t+1} - \bar r_i\right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\bar r_i$ is the mean return over the evaluation sample. A negative value
means that the model's squared error exceeds that of the constant. This reference
is stricter than the one used by \citet{campbell2008}, the estimation-sample mean,
because $\bar r_i$ is not known when the forecast is made. \emph{Directional
accuracy} is the share of days on which forecast and realised returns have the same
sign,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:da}
  \mathrm{DA}_i = \frac{1}{n_i}\sum_t \mathbf{1}\!\left[\operatorname{sign}\hat r_{i,t+1} = \operatorname{sign} r_{i,t+1}\right],
\end{equation}
with a day counted as up when its return is strictly positive. The \emph{edge} is
accuracy minus the accuracy of a rule that always predicts a rise, which equals the
share $b_i$ of up days in the evaluation sample:
$\mathrm{Edge}_i = \mathrm{DA}_i - b_i$. The always-up rule needs no model and is the
natural benchmark for a long-only investor, but its accuracy depends on the market:
it is high in a rising window and low in a flat or falling one.

The edge must therefore be read against the edge of a forecast with no information.
A forecast that predicts a rise with probability $q$, independently of the outcome,
has expected accuracy $qb_i + (1-q)(1-b_i)$. For $q = \tfrac12$ this is 50\% for
every stock, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:randomedge}
  \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Edge}_i \mid \text{uninformative forecast}\right] = \tfrac12 - b_i .
\end{equation}
Whenever up days are in the minority, a forecast with no information beats the
always-up rule by construction.

\subsection{Inference}

For each stock, a two-sided exact binomial test compares directional accuracy with
50\%. Because \NStocks{} hypotheses are tested together, the procedure of
\citet{benjamini1995} is applied to control the false discovery rate at 5\%. The
binomial test ignores the base rate of up days. The test of \citet{pesaran1992}
allows for it by comparing accuracy with the accuracy expected if forecasts and
outcomes were independent. With $p_y$ the share of up days, $p_x$ the share of days
on which the model forecasts a rise and $P^* = p_y p_x + (1-p_y)(1-p_x)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pt}
  \mathrm{PT}_i = \frac{\mathrm{DA}_i - P^*}{\sqrt{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\mathrm{DA}_i) - \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(P^*)}}
  \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0,1)
\end{equation}
under the null of no directional ability, with the variance estimators given by
\citet{pesaran1992}. The statistic is undefined when a model forecasts the same
sign on every day.

\subsection{Explained variance}

The mean out-of-sample $R^2$ is \RsqMean{} and the median \RsqMedian. All values lie
between \RsqMin{} and \RsqMax, and the \NRsqPos{} positive values are all small
(Figure~\ref{fig:fit}, Panel A). There is no tail of stocks on which the model
performs well. A negative $R^2$ for the typical stock means that the fitted model
forecasts next-day returns less accurately than a constant equal to the
evaluation-period mean. This is the outcome expected when the regressors carry no
information: the coefficients are fitted to noise in the estimation sample, and the
noise does not recur.

\subsection{Directional accuracy and the benchmark}

Mean directional accuracy is \AccMean, with a cross-sectional standard deviation of
\AccSD{} percentage points and a range of \AccMin{} to \AccMax. A $t$-test of the
cross-sectional mean against 50\% gives $t = \AccTstat$, and even this overstates
the evidence, because all stocks share one evaluation window and their errors are
correlated.

The model beats the always-up rule for \NEdgePos{} of \NStocks{} stocks, by
\EdgeMean{} percentage points on average (Figure~\ref{fig:fit}, Panel B). That
figure says more about the benchmark than about the model. Over the evaluation
window the median stock's price changed by \MedianTestChange{} and only \NRose{} of
\NStocks{} stocks rose, so up days were in the minority for \NWeakBench{} stocks and
the always-up rule averaged only \UpMean. By equation~\eqref{eq:randomedge}, a
forecast with no information would have beaten it by \RandomEdge{} points on
average, more than the model achieved. Figure~\ref{fig:edge} makes the comparison
stock by stock. The model's edge tracks the edge an uninformative forecast would
have earned, with a correlation of \EdgeBenchCorr{} across stocks, and the points
scatter around the 45-degree line rather than above it.

\subsection{Per-stock inference}\label{sec:perstock}

With a mean of \MeanTestDays{} evaluation days, the standard error of a single
stock's accuracy under the null of 50\% is about \SEAcc{} percentage points. In the
binomial tests, no stock is significantly above 50\% and \NSigBelow{} are
significantly below; roughly \ExpectedPerTail{} would be expected in each tail
by chance, and none of the \NSigBelow{} survives the Benjamini-Hochberg
correction. The Pesaran-Timmermann statistic, defined for \NPTDefined{} stocks
(one model forecast a fall on every day), has a median of \PTMedian. It is
significant at 5\% for \NPTSigPos{} stocks, all positive, which is close to the
number expected by chance among \NPTDefined{} tests, and for none after the same
correction.

A count of large edges seems to point the other way: \NOutsideBand{} stocks have an
edge outside a band of two standard errors around zero, \NAboveBand{} above and
\NBelowBand{} below (Figure~\ref{fig:ranked}). That count is misleading, because the
band is centred on zero while the edges are centred on \EdgeMean{} points for the
reason given above. The largest edges also tend to coincide with weak benchmarks
rather than with good fits: four of the five largest occur with a negative
$R^2_{OS}$ (Appendix~Table~\ref{tab:extremes}).

\subsection{The moving-average ratio}

The ratio has the largest standardised coefficient for none of the \NStocks{}
stocks; the largest is \textit{ma5} for \TopMaFive, \textit{ma20} for
\TopMaTwenty{} and the one-day return for \TopRet. Its mean absolute coefficient
is \RatioAbsMean, so a one standard deviation change in the ratio moves the
forecast daily return by about \RatioAbsMeanPP{} percentage points. The sign is
positive for \NRatioPos{} stocks and negative for \NRatioNeg. A sign test against
an even split gives $p = \SignTestP$, so the positive majority is unlikely to be
chance alone, but it has no forecasting value: the coefficients are small, and the
accuracy results above show no benefit. Figure~\ref{fig:ratio} shows the
coefficient for every stock in Panel A and compares the four features in Panel B.
The mean absolute coefficients on \textit{ma5} and \textit{ma20}, \MeanAbsMaFive{}
and \MeanAbsMaTwenty{} basis points per standard deviation, are about eight times
the \MeanAbsRatioBp{} on the ratio, but they are large mainly because they offset
each other.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ratio_coefficients.pdf}

The individual coefficients should be read with care. The ratio is a
deterministic function of \textit{ma5} and \textit{ma20}, which are themselves
highly correlated. Under this degree of multicollinearity, least squares can assign
large offsetting coefficients to correlated regressors with little change in the
fitted values, so the individual coefficients have high variance. The conclusions
of this paper rest on out-of-sample accuracy, which does not depend on how the
fitted values are divided among the regressors.

\section{Why the target is a return: a worked example}\label{sec:levels}

A natural alternative design forecasts the next day's \emph{price} rather than its
return. This section shows, for Reliance Industries, why that design produces a high
$R^2$ that is consistent with no forecasting ability. Two regressions with the same
features were fitted, one to the next day's close and one to its return, both
estimated on data from \RelTrainStart{} to \RelTrainEnd{} and evaluated on the
\RelTestDays{} trading days from \RelTestStart{} to \RelTestEnd. A third
forecast, the random walk $\hat P_{t+1} = P_t$, requires no estimation.

The price model attains an $R^2$ of \RelPriceRsq, the kind of figure that suggests a
strong model (Table~\ref{tab:reliance}). The random walk attains \RelNaiveRsq, with
a mean absolute error \RelMAEcut{} lower, and it beats the price model on every
measure in the table. The reason is in the denominator of $R^2$, which compares the
squared forecast errors with the variance of the outcome around its mean. For a price
level that variance is large relative to a single day's change. Over the evaluation
sample the close had a standard deviation of \rupee\RelCloseSD, while the random
walk's root mean squared error, the typical size of a daily move, was
\rupee\RelRWRMSE. The random walk therefore scores
$R^2 = 1 - (\RelRWRMSE/\RelCloseSD)^2 = \RelNaiveRsq$ without forecasting anything,
and any forecast close to the previous close explains most of the variance of the
level, whether or not it anticipates the change.

The last row of Table~\ref{tab:reliance} scores direction, and there the high $R^2$
counts for nothing. The price model calls the next day's move correctly on
\RelPriceDir{} of days, worse than a coin. The random walk never forecasts a rise,
so its \RelNaiveDir{} is simply the share of days on which the price did not rise.
The return model is right on \RelReturnDir{} of days against \RelUpShare{} for the
always-up rule, an edge of \RelEdge{} points, well inside the band of
$\pm$\TwoSE{} points within which an uninformative forecast falls
(Section~\ref{sec:perstock}).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/reliance_series.pdf}
\caption{Reliance Industries over the evaluation period. Panel A shows the realised
close and the price model's forecast, Panel B the realised daily return and the
return model's forecast, and Panel C the return model's residuals, realised minus
forecast.}
\label{fig:relseries}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:relseries} shows the two forecasts over the evaluation period. In
Panel A the forecast close follows the realised close so closely that the two lines
are hard to separate, because it is in effect yesterday's close. Forecasting the
return removes the level, and the apparent fit goes with it. In Panel B the return
forecast barely leaves zero while the realised return swings between \RelActLo{}
and \RelActHi. Because the forecasts are close to zero, the residuals in Panel C
are close to the realised returns themselves, and they show no structure that the
model has removed.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/reliance_parity.pdf}
\caption{Forecast against realised values for Reliance Industries over the same
\RelTestDays{} evaluation days. Panel A is the price model and Panel B the return
model; the dashed line marks perfect forecasts. The level forecasts lie close to the
line because prices change little from one day to the next. The return forecasts
form a narrow vertical band, which indicates that they do not vary with the
outcome.}
\label{fig:parity}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:parity} plots each forecast against the outcome. The level
forecasts lie along the diagonal, while the return forecasts form a narrow vertical
band within \RelPredBand{} of zero. A near-constant forecast is what least squares
produces when the regressors are uninformative: the estimated coefficients are
close to zero, and the fitted value converges to the estimation-sample mean, which
for a large stock's daily return is itself close to zero.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/reliance_coefficients.pdf}
\caption{The standardised coefficients of the two Reliance models: rupees of
forecast close per standard deviation of each feature for the price model, and
basis points of forecast return per standard deviation for the return model.}
\label{fig:relcoef}
\end{figure}

The coefficients show how each model reaches its forecast
(Figure~\ref{fig:relcoef}). The price model puts almost all its weight on
\textit{ma5}, the feature closest to today's price, so it forecasts tomorrow's
price mainly by reproducing today's. In the return model the two largest
coefficients, on \textit{ma5} and \textit{ma20}, are nearly equal and opposite.
Because the two averages move together, their effects cancel on a typical day,
which is how correlated regressors behave when they are fitted to noise.

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/fitted_plane.pdf}
\caption{A two-feature slice of the fitted Reliance return model, with the one-day
return and the moving-average ratio held at their means. The plane is drawn only
over the range of combinations that occur in the data. The points are the realised
returns of the evaluation days.}
\label{fig:plane}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:plane} draws the return model as a surface. With the one-day
return and the ratio held at their means, the forecast is a plane in \textit{ma5}
and \textit{ma20}, and the vertical distance from each day to the plane is the
error that least squares minimises. The observations lie along a narrow diagonal
band, because the two averages rarely move apart, and along that band the plane is
almost flat while the realised returns scatter far above and below it. A model with
real predictive power would show a plane tilted along the band, with the days
close to it.

The general point is that $R^2$ measures variance explained relative to a chosen
reference forecast. For an autocorrelated series such as a price level, the
appropriate reference is the random walk, not the sample mean.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The results are consistent with the weak form of the efficient market hypothesis
\citep{fama1970}, under which current prices reflect the information contained in
past prices. A rule based on past prices that reliably preceded price increases
would be traded until the return it offered was competed away. Moving averages are
among the most widely computed indicators, so whatever predictability they once
carried is likely to have been exploited, and the decline in the profitability of
technical rules after the early 1990s documented by \citet{park2007} points the
same way.

The evidence is limited in a way that matters for interpretation. With about 345
evaluation days, a single stock would need directional accuracy of roughly \MDEAcc{}
for a two-sided test at 5\% to detect it with 80\% power. Effects of one or two
percentage points, which could still be economically meaningful for a large
portfolio traded at low cost, cannot be detected stock by stock. Pooling across
stocks helps less than the count of \NStocks{} suggests, because the stocks share a
single evaluation window. What the data can rule out is a large and general effect:
there is no subset of stocks, capitalisation band or feature for which the model
performs reliably better than an uninformative forecast.

The results do not contradict the momentum literature. \citet{jegadeesh1993}
document that stocks with high returns over the previous three to twelve months
outperform those with low returns over the following months. That effect is
measured across stocks and over monthly horizons. This paper measures the
relationship between short moving averages and the next day's return for each stock
separately, which is a different hypothesis.

The conclusion is also narrow in what it covers. It rules out a one-day forecast
built from these four features with a linear model, on this universe and in this
period, which is the horizon on which short-term technical trading operates. It
says nothing about investing on fundamentals. Profitability, reinvestment,
leverage, cash flow and valuation are not inputs to the model, so the results
neither support nor contradict their use.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{One evaluation window.} All \NStocks{} evaluation samples fall within
  the same seventeen months, February 2025 to June 2026, so the results for
  different stocks are correlated, and a
  window with different market conditions could produce a different benchmark and
  a different edge.
\item \textit{Specification.} The model is linear in four features. A relationship
  that operates through thresholds or interactions, such as a crossing that
  predicts returns only in calm markets, would not be detected.
\item \textit{Corporate actions.} Price adjustment does not correct the two
  demergers in the evaluation window (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item \textit{Returns and costs.} Returns exclude dividends, and no transaction
  costs are deducted. A small positive edge before costs would not imply a
  profitable strategy after brokerage, the bid-ask spread and short-term capital
  gains tax.
\item \textit{Universe.} Most of the stocks are large and mid capitalisation
  constituents, among the most widely followed Indian equities, so a null result is
  less surprising here than it would be among less liquid stocks. Capitalisation
  bands are fixed as of June 2021.
\end{itemize}

% ======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Four moving-average features in a linear model do not forecast the next day's
return of \NStocks{} NSE stocks better than benchmarks that use no model. The mean
out-of-sample $R^2$ is negative, mean directional accuracy is \AccMean, and no stock
shows significant directional ability once the base rate of up days and the number
of tests are taken into account. The apparent edge over an always-up rule is an
artefact of a flat evaluation window, and it is smaller than the edge an
uninformative forecast would have earned. Two lessons about method generalise
beyond this sample: a benchmark's accuracy must be read alongside the conditions
that produced it, and $R^2$ on an autocorrelated level is not evidence of
forecasting ability.

Natural extensions are cross-sectional momentum at monthly horizons, a larger
small-capitalisation sample, predictors that do not depend on price history, such
as earnings revisions, and non-linear models evaluated with walk-forward
validation. Replacing the ratio with an indicator for a recent crossing would remove
its collinearity with the two averages and test the rule in the form in which it is
used.
